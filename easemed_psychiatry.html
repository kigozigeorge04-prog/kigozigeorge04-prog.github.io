🧠 What is the Mental State Examination (MSE)?

The MSE is the psychiatric equivalent of a physical examination. It is a structured way of observing and describing a patient's current mental state, based on both objective signs observed by the clinician and subjective information reported by the patient.

🎯 The goal is to describe what you observe objectively. Avoid making early inferences or diagnoses during the assessment itself.

📋 The ASEPTIC Framework

The MSE is traditionally organized using the ASEPTIC mnemonic. Here's what to assess in each domain:

A — Appearance & Behaviour
  • Appearance: Sex, apparent age vs stated age, any physical abnormalities, level of grooming, level of cleanliness
  • Behaviour: Mode of entry, cooperativeness, eye-to-eye contact, rapport, attitude (cooperative, guarded, hostile)
  • Psychomotor activity: Any agitation (restlessness, fidgeting) or retardation (slowed movements), posture, gestures
  • Level of consciousness: Alert, drowsy, confused, fluctuating
💡 Example: "The patient is a 45-year-old man who appears his stated age. He is dressed in a tracksuit and appears unkempt with poor hygiene. He maintained poor eye contact and was guarded throughout the interview. No abnormal movements noted."
S — Speech
  • Initiation: Spontaneous or requires prompting
  • Reaction time: Normal, prolonged, or absent
  • Rate: Pressured (fast, difficult to interrupt), slow, or normal
  • Productivity: Amount of speech (verbose, minimal)
  • Volume: Loud, soft, or mumbled
  • Tone & Rhythm: Monotone, hesitant, or fluent
  • Relevance: Relevant to questions or tangential
  • Stream: Continuous or interrupted
  • Coherence: Logical and understandable or disorganised
E — Emotion (Mood & Affect)
  • Mood (Subjective): What the patient reports. Ask: "How have you been feeling in yourself over the past few weeks?" Document in their own words (e.g., "Patient describes mood as 'rock bottom'").
  • Affect (Objective): What you observe.
    • Quality: Euthymic (normal), depressed, elevated/euphoric, anxious, irritable, or flat/blunted
    • Range & Stability: Is the affect reactive to the conversation? Or is it fixed and unchanging? Is it labile (rapidly shifting)?
    • Congruence: Is the affect appropriate for what the patient is saying (congruent) or inappropriate (e.g., laughing while describing a sad event)?
📝 Key Distinction: Mood = subjective (patient's report). Affect = objective (clinician's observation).
P — Perception
  • Hallucinations: Perceptions without a stimulus. Can be:
    • Auditory (most common) — ask: "Do you hear things that others don't, like voices?" Clarify if voices are commenting or giving commands.
    • Visual
    • Olfactory (smell)
    • Gustatory (taste)
    • Tactile (touch)
  • Illusions: Misperceptions of a real stimulus (e.g., seeing a coat and thinking it's a person).
  • Depersonalization/Derealization: Feelings of being detached from oneself or the world feeling unreal.
🚨 Red Flag: Command hallucinations with compliance — major safety concern requiring immediate intervention.
T — Thought (Content & Process)
  • Thought Form (Process): The flow and organization of ideas.
    • Normal: Coherent, logical, and goal-directed.
    • Abnormalities: Flight of ideas, loosening of associations, tangentiality, circumstantiality, thought blocking, neologisms (made-up words).
  • Thought Content: The themes of the patient's ideas and beliefs.
    • Delusions: Fixed, false beliefs not shared by culture (e.g., paranoid, grandiose, persecutory, nihilistic, guilt).
    • Overvalued Ideas: Strong beliefs held with less conviction than a delusion.
    • Obsessions: Recurrent, intrusive thoughts.
    • Suicidal/Homicidal Ideation: Essential to assess for thoughts of harm to self or others.
🚨 Red Flag: Suicidal ideation with plan and intent — requires immediate safety intervention.
I — Insight & Judgement
  • Insight: The patient's awareness and understanding of their own mental health condition. Ask: "Do you feel you need any treatment?" or "What do you think is the cause of your problems?" Can be graded as:
    • Complete: Understands condition and need for treatment
    • Partial: Some awareness but limited understanding
    • Absent: No awareness of illness
  • Judgement: The patient's ability to make sound decisions and foresee consequences. Ask situational questions like: "What would you do if you smelled smoke in a crowded room?" Often inferred from their history and actions.
C — Cognition
  • Orientation: Assess for person, place, and time.
    • Person: Oriented in self, close associates, hospital staff
    • Place: Knows where they are
    • Time: Knows date, day, time
  • Attention: Normally aroused, aroused with difficulty, or inattentive to examiner.
  • Concentration: Normally sustained, sustained with difficulty, distractible. Test with:
    • Serial 7s or 3s from 100
    • Count backwards from 20
    • Spell "WORLD" backwards
  • Memory: Assess Immediate (digit span), Recent (what they ate for breakfast), and Remote (past events, personal history).
  • Intelligence: General fund of information, arithmetic ability. Questions should be appropriate to the patient's age and level of education.
  • Abstract Thinking: Interpretation of proverbs, similarities between paired objects (e.g., table and chair).
💡 Tip: For cognitive assessment, ensure questions are appropriate to the patient's age and educational background.

🧬 Bipolar Disorders — DSM-5-TR Criteria

Bipolar I Disorder — Mania
  • Duration: ≥ 7 days (or any duration if hospitalization is required)
  • Core Feature: At least one manic episode
  • Symptoms (≥3 of the following):
    • Inflated self-esteem or grandiosity
    • Decreased need for sleep
    • More talkative than usual
    • Flight of ideas or racing thoughts
    • Distractibility
    • Increase in goal-directed activity or psychomotor agitation
    • Excessive involvement in high-risk activities
  • Psychosis: May occur during manic episodes
  • Functional Impairment: Severe; often leads to hospitalization
  • Course: Episodic with periods of mania and/or depression
📌 DIFGAST Mnemonic for Mania:
D — Distractibility
I — Impulsivity / Risky Behavior
F — Flight of Ideas
G — Grandiosity
A — Activity Increase
S — Sleep Deficit
T — Talkativeness
Major Depressive Episode
  • Duration: ≥ 2 weeks
  • Symptoms (≥5 of the following):
    • Depressed mood
    • Loss of interest or pleasure (anhedonia)
    • Change in appetite or weight
    • Sleep disturbance
    • Fatigue or loss of energy
    • Feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt
    • Difficulty concentrating or indecisiveness
    • Recurrent thoughts of death or suicidal ideation
  • Functional Impairment: Significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or other areas

🦿 Physiotherapy Referral in Mental Health

All mental health inpatients should be screened to identify those with physiotherapy needs.

  • Findings relevant to physiotherapy should be documented in the patient's file
  • Communicate physiotherapy needs during ward rounds
  • Engage the physiotherapy team for further assessment and tailored management
Indications for Physiotherapy Referral:
  • Reduced mobility due to side effects from medications
  • Postural instability — risk of falls
  • Psychomotor slowing or agitation
  • Chronic pain
  • Deconditioning from prolonged hospitalization (Harrison et al., 2024)

⚡ Seizure Assessment — Essential Questions

When someone has reported having a seizure, ask:

  1. Was any warning noted before the seizure? If so, what kind?
  2. What did the patient do during the seizure?
  3. Was the patient able to relate to the environment during the seizure? Does the patient have recollection of the seizure?
  4. How did the patient feel after the seizure? How long did it take to get back to baseline?
  5. How long did the seizure last?
  6. How frequently do the seizures occur?
  7. Is anything known to precipitate the seizures?
  8. Has the patient shown any response to therapy for the seizures?

— Innovated by Abakunda Annet, 2016/BNC/001/PS

💡 Key Tips for OSCE Success

  • Practice the Performance: Knowing the theory is not enough. The OSCE is a performance exam. Practice presenting the MSE findings out loud, in the correct order, until it becomes fluid and automatic.
  • Use Clear Clinical Language: Avoid vague terms like "looks a bit down." Use precise terminology: "Appears dishevelled with a depressed affect."
  • Structure Matters: Examiners reward a structured, logical presentation. Following the ASEPTIC order demonstrates your clinical reasoning and command of the examination.
  • Always assess risk first: Screen for suicidal ideation and risk to others before proceeding with the full history.
  • Document in patient's own words: For hallucinations and delusions, use direct quotes where possible.
  • Consider organic causes: Always rule out delirium, infection, metabolic derangement, and substance use in new-onset presentations.
📝 Remember: The MSE is a snapshot in time — it describes the patient's mental state at the time of the interview, not their entire personality or history.
